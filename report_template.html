Methodology

WAAS-capable GPS receivers offer advisory vertical guidance on RNAV (GPS) approaches that have only LNAV minimums. It shows up as “+V” on the annunciator, e.g., “LNAV+V”. This glidepath is not part of the FAA’s published procedure. It is a single straight line that ends at the runway’s threshold crossing height (TCH). Its angle is normally whatever is needed to cross the Final Approach Fix (FAF) at the FAF altitude. If that line would pass below the altitude of a stepdown fix, the angle is made steeper, just enough to clear the fix. Unlike the published procedure, though, the glidepath has not been evaluated for obstacle clearance. Between fixes, it can descend through terrain that the published stepdown altitudes and the MDA were designed to clear.

This study looks for approaches that have this problem. For every RNAV (GPS) approach in the FAA’s database that has no published Vertical Descent Angle, the terrain is sampled every 30 m along the ground track from the FAF to the threshold and compared to the altitude of the +V glidepath at that point. An approach makes the list if the glidepath comes within 250′ of the terrain, which is the minimum obstacle clearance that TERPS requires in the final approach segment.

The glidepath is a straight line in space, and the earth curves away underneath it. Its altitude above sea level is therefore a little higher than a flat-earth calculation would give: about 22′ higher at 5 NM from the threshold, and 88′ at 10 NM. This is included. One consequence is that the glidepath is often still above the FAF altitude at the FAF, especially when a stepdown fix has made it steeper. An aircraft crossing the FAF at the FAF altitude is assumed to fly level until it meets the glidepath, and then follow it down.

The last 0.8 NM before the threshold is not checked. Any glidepath to a runway has to get within 250′ of the ground eventually: over flat terrain, a 3° glidepath is at 250′ AGL about 0.8 NM out. Counting those points would put nearly every approach on the list.

The +V Clearance column is the smallest gap between the +V glidepath and the terrain anywhere along the final approach segment. The Procedure Clearance column is the terrain clearance at that same spot for a pilot who ignores the +V and flies the published procedure instead, i.e., the stepdown altitudes and then the MDA. The difference between the two columns is the margin that the published procedure provides and the +V glidepath does not.

Each approach has a Profile chart that shows it in cross-section. The red line is the altitude of an aircraft following the +V glidepath from the FAF to the threshold. If a stepdown fix sets the glidepath angle, the legend says which one. The blue lines are the published stepdown altitudes and the MDA. Terrain is brown, and turns red wherever the +V glidepath is less than 250′ above it. Yellow marks the final 0.8 NM of the approach, where violations are no longer checked. The purple vertical line labeled Proc Clearance is drawn at the point of minimum +V clearance; its height is the clearance you’d have there if you were flying the published procedure.

Terrain elevations come from the Shuttle Radar Topography Mission (SRTM), a near-global elevation dataset that NASA collected from the Space Shuttle in 2000. The SRTM1 version is used here, which has a grid spacing of 1 arc-second, or about 30 m. Each sample point gets the elevation of the grid cell it falls in, with no interpolation. Keep in mind that SRTM is a radar measurement of the surface, not of bare earth. Over forests and towns it reports heights close to the treetops or rooftops, so some of what is called “terrain” here is really trees and buildings. It can’t see narrow obstacles like towers, and of course it knows nothing about anything built or grown since 2000.

The approaches themselves (fix coordinates, altitudes, and threshold crossing heights) come from the FAA’s Coded Instrument Flight Procedures (CIFP), which is published in ARINC 424 format. The cycle used is shown at the top of this page. The CIFP doesn’t include MDAs, so those come from Ammar Askar’s FAA instrument approach database, which reads them off of the published approach plates and may be a cycle or more behind.

Changes from the first version

The first version of this page assumed that the +V glidepath always ran straight from the FAF altitude to the TCH, even if that took it below a stepdown fix. A reader wrote in to say that Garmin’s avionics don’t do that: they steepen the glidepath to stay above the stepdown fixes. That turned out to be right. The glidepath was measured on four approaches in Garmin’s GTN 750Xi trainer, and on each one its angle matched the description above to within 0.01°. The same measurements showed the effect of the earth’s curvature, which the first version also left out. Receivers from other manufacturers have not been tested and may behave differently.

The first version listed 75 approaches; this one lists 74. Two came off because of these corrections: KSMQ RNAV (GPS) RWY 30, where the steeper angle raised the clearance from 231′ to 320′, and F17 RNAV (GPS) RWY 17, which went from 249′ to 250′. The clearance at 4V0 RNAV (GPS) RWY 25 went from 79′ to 105′. Every other clearance changed by 10′ or less; KSBS, for example, went from −108′ to −99′.

The rest of the difference is not from the glidepath. The first version used FAA data from February 2026. Since then, the FAA has published a descent angle for KOQW RNAV (GPS) RWY 15, which takes it off of the list, and removed the one for 09A RNAV (GPS) RWY 12, which puts it on. The first version also had a bug that left 29 procedures unexamined. One of them, 6P7 RNAV (GPS) RWY 32, is now on the list.
