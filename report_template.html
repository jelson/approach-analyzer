Methodology

WAAS-capable GPS receivers offer advisory vertical guidance on RNAV (GPS) approaches that have only LNAV minimums. It shows up as “+V” on the annunciator, e.g., “LNAV+V”. This glidepath is not part of the FAA’s published procedure. The receiver computes it on its own, as a straight line from the altitude of the Final Approach Fix (FAF) to the runway’s threshold crossing height (TCH). It ignores stepdown fixes, and unlike the published procedure, it has not been evaluated for obstacle clearance. That means it can descend through terrain that the published stepdown altitudes were designed to clear.

This study looks for approaches that have this problem. For every RNAV (GPS) approach in the FAA’s database that has no published Vertical Descent Angle, the terrain is sampled every 30 m along the ground track from the FAF to the threshold and compared to the altitude of the +V glidepath at that point. An approach makes the list if the glidepath comes within 250′ of the terrain, which is the minimum obstacle clearance that TERPS requires in the final approach segment.

The last 0.8 NM before the threshold is not checked. Any glidepath to a runway has to get within 250′ of the ground eventually: over flat terrain, a 3° glidepath is at 250′ AGL about 0.8 NM out. Counting those points would put nearly every approach on the list.

The +V Clearance column is the smallest gap between the +V glidepath and the terrain anywhere along the final approach segment. The Procedure Clearance column is the terrain clearance at that same spot for a pilot who ignores the +V and flies the published procedure instead, i.e., the stepdown altitudes and then the MDA. The difference between the two columns is the margin that the published procedure provides and the +V glidepath does not.

Each approach has a Profile chart that shows it in cross-section. The red line is the +V glidepath from the FAF to the threshold. The blue lines are the published stepdown altitudes and the MDA. Terrain is brown, and turns red wherever the +V glidepath is less than 250′ above it. Yellow marks the final 0.8 NM of the approach, where violations are no longer checked. The purple vertical line labeled Proc Clearance is drawn at the point of minimum +V clearance; its height is the clearance you’d have there if you were flying the published procedure.

Terrain elevations come from the Shuttle Radar Topography Mission (SRTM), a near-global elevation dataset that NASA collected from the Space Shuttle in 2000. The SRTM1 version is used here, which has a grid spacing of 1 arc-second, or about 30 m. Each sample point gets the elevation of the grid cell it falls in, with no interpolation. Keep in mind that SRTM is a radar measurement of the surface, not of bare earth. Over forests and towns it reports heights close to the treetops or rooftops, so some of what is called “terrain” here is really trees and buildings. It can’t see narrow obstacles like towers, and of course it knows nothing about anything built or grown since 2000.

The approaches themselves (fix coordinates, altitudes, and threshold crossing heights) come from the FAA’s Coded Instrument Flight Procedures (CIFP), which is published in ARINC 424 format. The cycle used is shown at the top of this page. The CIFP doesn’t include MDAs, so those come from Ammar Askar’s FAA instrument approach database, which reads them off of the published approach plates and may be a cycle or more behind.
